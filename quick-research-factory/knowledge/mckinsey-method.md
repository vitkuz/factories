# The research method, simplified

This is the way a strategy-consulting team researches a question, cut down to the six
habits that matter. Every step of a research run reads this file, so it says what all of
them share; the shape of each step's own output is in its own file beside this one.

## 1. Start with the question, not the topic

A topic is not researchable. "Electric trucks" is a topic; "Should a mid-size fleet operator
in Poland switch to electric trucks before 2028?" is a question. The first job is to turn the
topic into one **governing question** that a reader could answer yes, no, or with a number.
Everything after exists to answer that question and nothing else.

## 2. Guess the answer first, then try to break it

Write the **hypothesis** before searching: the answer you would bet on today, in one
sentence. Research then becomes a test of that sentence. This is faster than open-ended
reading because it tells you what evidence would matter. A hypothesis that survives is the
answer; a hypothesis that dies is replaced, and the report says it died and why.

## 3. Break the question into MECE pieces

Split the governing question into sub-questions that are **mutually exclusive** (no two
overlap) and **collectively exhaustive** (together they cover the whole question). This is
the issue tree. Each sub-question gets its own hypothesis and its own evidence. If answering
all the sub-questions would not answer the governing question, the tree is incomplete; if
two sub-questions need the same evidence, they are one sub-question.

A useful test: read the sub-questions aloud and ask "and if I knew all of that, would I
know the answer?"

## 4. Evidence beats opinion, and every number has a source

A claim without a source is a guess wearing a suit. Every fact carries the URL it came from
and the date it was read. Prefer, in this order: primary data (a company filing, a statute,
a dataset, an official statistic), a peer-reviewed or institutional study, reporting from a
named outlet, an expert's own words, and last a secondary summary. Two sources that disagree
are recorded as disagreement, not averaged and not resolved by picking the nicer one.

## 5. Write answer first (the pyramid)

A reader with five minutes gets the answer in the first paragraph, the three or four reasons
under it, and the evidence under each reason. Never build up to a conclusion. Every section
opens with its "so what" in one sentence, and the detail below supports that sentence.
A heading is a claim ("Demand doubles by 2027"), not a label ("Demand").

## 6. Apply the 80/20 and stop

Most of the answer comes from a few facts. Find those first, get them right, and say what
you did not verify rather than spend the run on the last twenty percent. A known gap stated
plainly is worth more than a paragraph that hides it. The output of a step is done when a
skeptical reader would trust it, not when there is nothing more to add.

## Words to use consistently

| Word | Meaning here |
|------|--------------|
| governing question | the one question the whole run answers |
| hypothesis | the one-sentence answer you would bet on before the evidence is in |
| issue tree | the governing question split into MECE sub-questions |
| sub-question | one branch of the issue tree, with its own hypothesis and evidence |
| finding | one sourced fact or observation that bears on a sub-question |
| so what | the one sentence a reader should take from a section |
| known gap | a fact the answer needed that the run could not verify |
