# Executive Writing

Write so that a busy decision-maker gets the answer in 30 seconds and the case for it in 5 minutes.
The Minto Pyramid Principle says ideas are easiest to grasp when they are organized as a pyramid under a single point.

## Pyramid Principle
- **Answer first.** Open with the governing thought, which answers the key question. Supporting arguments come next, then evidence.
- **Vertical logic (question and answer).** Each point raises a question in the reader's mind ("why?" or "how?"). The points directly below it answer that question, and only that question.
- **Horizontal logic.** The ideas in a group are either:
  - *Inductive:* parallel items of the same kind, nameable by one plural noun (3 reasons, 4 steps, 3 risks). Prefer this at the top level, because the reader gets the point at once.
  - *Deductive:* a chain ("X; and Y; therefore Z"). Use it sparingly and keep it short, because the reader has to hold every step in mind.
- **Groups are MECE**, hold 3–5 items, and are ordered logically (by time, structure, or importance).
- **Test the pyramid (staff paper):**
  - Down: does each point pose one question that the group below fully answers?
  - Across: is each level MECE?
  - Up: does each group sum to exactly one "so what?", the point above it?

## SCQA opening
Open the document, and the executive summary, with:
- **Situation:** what the reader already knows and agrees with. Keep it short.
- **Complication:** what changed or what is at stake.
- **Question:** the key question that follows (it can be implied).
- **Answer:** the recommendation, in one sentence.

Example: "Segment S grew 14% a year to EUR 400M (S). Two incumbents now bundle a free tool, so paid share is capped (C). Should we enter (Q)? Yes, via a partner channel with a freemium tier, targeting EUR 8–14M revenue in year 3 (A)."

## Action titles
- Every section heading and slide title is a **full-sentence takeaway**, not a topic:
  - Topic: "Market size".
  - Action title: "The reachable market is EUR 60–90M, too small to justify a direct sales force."
- Read in order, the titles alone must tell the whole storyline (the ghost-deck test).
- One message per section or slide. The body proves the title and nothing else.
- Keep titles to about 2 lines at most. Use specific numbers, not adjectives.

## Executive summary (≤1 page)
1. SCQA paragraph ending in the recommendation.
2. 3–5 bullets with the key insights, each an action-title sentence with its headline number and confidence.
3. The recommended actions and first steps: owner and timing.
4. Top 2–3 risks, each with a mitigation.
5. What we are uncertain about, and what would change the answer.

It must stand alone. A reader who stops here has what they need to decide.

## Structure of the final decision report
1. **Executive summary** (≤1 page, as above).
2. **Recommendation:** the decision, its scale and timing, and the conditions attached.
3. **Key insights with evidence:** one section per insight. Action title, argument, quantified implication, evidence cited inline (claim · source · date · grade).
4. **Options considered:** the alternatives, the criteria, and why they were rejected.
5. **Risks & mitigations:** likelihood, impact, mitigation, early-warning indicator.
6. **Assumptions:** the linchpin assumptions, what happens if each is wrong, and the indicator to watch.
7. **Open questions & gaps:** what is unknown, how much it matters, and how to close it.
8. **Next steps:** sequenced actions, owners, decision gates, leading indicators.
9. **Appendices:** detailed research by lens, calculations and sensitivity tables, and the full source list with grades.

## Plain-language style rules
- Put the conclusion in the first sentence of every section and paragraph.
- Use short sentences and active voice. Name the actor: "We recommend", "Competitor X will likely".
- Use concrete nouns and numbers. Cut filler ("it is important to note", "leverage", "synergies", "robust").
- Define each acronym once. Use one term for each concept throughout.
- Use bullets for parallel items, prose for arguments. Use a table when comparing more than 2 items on more than 2 attributes.
- Every chart or table needs a takeaway title and a stated source.
- Don't hedge sentence by sentence. State the uncertainty once, precisely, where it matters.

## Presenting numbers and uncertainty
- Give ranges with a base case: "EUR 8–14M (base 11M)". Round to the precision the evidence supports.
- Always give the units, currency, year basis and definition ("net revenue, 2026 EUR").
- Put the comparison beside the number: vs last year, vs competitor, vs threshold in the success criteria.
- Mark claim types where it matters: fact, estimate, assumption or opinion.
- Use consistent likelihood words, e.g. the ICD 203 scale:

| Term | Probability |
|------|-------------|
| almost no chance | 1–5% |
| very unlikely | 5–20% |
| unlikely | 20–45% |
| roughly even chance | 45–55% |
| likely | 55–80% |
| very likely | 80–95% |
| almost certain(ly) | 95–99% |

- Keep **likelihood** (how probable an event is) separate from **confidence** (how solid the evidence is). Don't combine them in one sentence.
- Name the causes of uncertainty (thin data, conflicting sources, novel market) and the indicators that would change the assessment.

## Final checklist
- [ ] First sentence answers the key question.
- [ ] Executive summary is ≤1 page and stands alone.
- [ ] Titles alone tell the story.
- [ ] Every number has a unit, a date, a source and a range where it is uncertain.
- [ ] Options, risks, assumptions and open questions are all present.
- [ ] Nothing is included that doesn't support the governing thought. Detail goes in the appendices.
