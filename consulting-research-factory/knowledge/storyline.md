# How the storyline is shaped

The storyline is the report written as a skeleton: every heading and exhibit title in order,
with the evidence under each. It is reviewed before a word of prose exists, because a bad
storyline cannot be rescued by good writing. The form is Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle,
written as a dot-dash outline.

## The pyramid rules

1. Every idea summarizes the ideas grouped below it.
2. Ideas in a group are the same kind of idea (all reasons, all steps, all segments).
3. Ideas in a group are in a stated logical order: time, structure, or importance.
4. Groups hold three to five ideas. Never more than seven.
5. **Vertical logic:** each statement raises a question (why? how? so what?) and the level below
   answers exactly that question.
6. **Horizontal logic:** the key line is preferably **inductive** — parallel reasons that each
   support the answer, so one failed point leaves the others standing. Use a deductive chain
   (premise → premise → therefore) only when the logic truly is a chain.

## Sections, in this order

1. **SCQA introduction**
   - *Situation* — what the reader already agrees with.
   - *Complication* — what changed, why it matters now.
   - *Question* — the governing question.
   - *Answer* — the governing thought, one sentence.
2. **Title and dek** — a report title in the form "Main phrase: subtitle", and a one-sentence
   dek carrying the headline number.
3. **At a glance (draft)** — five bullets. Each opens with a one-sentence claim, then one or two
   sentences with the number and the mechanism. At least four carry a number or a range; the
   last is the implication or call to action.
4. **Dot-dash storyline** — the whole argument:

   ```
   • Governing thought
     • Key message 1 — full-sentence action title (becomes chapter 1 heading)
       – Supporting point, with number-register keys / evidence ids
       – Supporting point …
       ▸ Exhibit 1: <action title> — <chart type> — <number-register keys>
     • Key message 2 …
   ```

   Three to five key messages. Each key message is a chapter. Each chapter has one to three
   exhibits. The last chapter is "what this means for <the decision maker>" with the
   recommendations.
5. **Exhibit list** — every exhibit in order: number, action title (15 words or fewer, with a
   number), the comparison it makes (component, item, time series, distribution, correlation),
   the chart form, the number-register keys it plots, and the one sentence the text will say
   about it.
6. **Key questions for leaders** — four to six questions or bold imperatives the closing
   chapter will pose to the decision maker, each tied to a recommendation.
7. **Titles-only test** — all the headings and exhibit titles listed in order, alone. Read them
   top to bottom: do they tell the full argument and land on the recommendation? State that
   they do, or fix them until they do.
8. **Changes from the ghost deck** — which expected titles survived, which died, and why. A
   storyline identical to the ghost deck is suspicious; say why the evidence confirmed it.

## Rules

- Every heading is a claim with a subject and a verb, not a label. "Enterprise spend could triple
  by 2030, but four use cases take most of it", not "Market overview".
- Every supporting point cites number-register keys or evidence ids. A point with none is cut.
- Only the synthesis's insights appear. The storyline organizes; it does not discover.
- The governing thought passes the 30-second test and answers the governing question as asked.
