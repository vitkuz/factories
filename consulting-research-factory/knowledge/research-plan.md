# How the research plan and the ghost deck are shaped

Two files. The **research plan** says which analysis tests which hypothesis, where the data is
and who runs it. The **ghost deck** is the report's skeleton before any data: the titles the
study expects to write, so a wrong storyline is found early. "We don't do any analyses that
aren't guided by very clear and testable hypotheses." (Conn & McLean.)

## The three research lenses

The plan splits the work across three analysts who run in parallel and must not overlap:

| Lens | Owns |
|------|------|
| **numbers** | market sizes and growth, prices, costs, unit economics, financials, official statistics, adoption rates; market sizing top-down and bottom-up |
| **players** | companies and competitors, their products, pricing and strategy; customers and buyers; regulators and policy; funding and M&A; case examples; what named leaders and experts said |
| **counter-evidence** | for every priority-1 and priority-2 hypothesis: the kill test, bear cases, failed attempts, contrary data, alternative explanations, the strongest rival hypothesis |

Every workplan row has exactly one lens. A row that needs two is two rows.

## The research plan — sections

1. **Governing question and day-one answer** — copied.
2. **Workplan** — one table, ordered by priority, kill tests first:

   | # | Hypothesis | Analysis | Lens | End product (dummy exhibit + expected title) | Likely sources | Search queries | Kill? |
   |---|-----------|----------|------|-----------------------------------------------|----------------|----------------|-------|

   - **Analysis** — what is computed or compared, precisely: "global spend 2024–2030 by segment,
     two independent estimates, reconciled", not "look at the market".
   - **End product** — the exhibit it would feed, with the chart type and the title you expect
     if the hypothesis holds. Dummying the chart first proves the analysis matters.
   - **Likely sources** — named where possible: "Eurostat ICT survey", "company 10-Ks", "Gartner
     press releases (T3 unless the method is published)".
   - **Search queries** — three to five per row, ready to paste. Vary them: one plain, one with a
     year, one naming a likely source, one phrased as the opposite claim. Use the language of the
     sources most likely to hold the answer.
   - **Kill?** — yes when the row is the hypothesis's kill test.
3. **Market sizing approach** — where the question involves a market: the top-down route
   (total → filters) and the bottom-up route (unit × count), each as a formula with the inputs
   the numbers analyst must find.
4. **Stop rule** — how the analysts know they are done: every row has an answer or a stated gap;
   8–20 evidence records per lens is normal; stop at the 80 percent.
5. **Known risks to the plan** — data likely to be missing or paywalled, and the fallback.

## The ghost deck — sections

1. **Governing thought (expected)** — the day-one answer rewritten as the report's top line.
2. **Storyline (expected)** — three to five key messages as full-sentence action titles, with
   placeholder numbers in brackets: "Enterprise agent spend could reach [$X–Y]B by 2030, [N]x
   today". Under each, two to four planned exhibits: number, action title, chart type, the
   workplan rows that feed it.
3. **What exists / what is missing** — for each exhibit, whether public data is likely to exist.
4. **What would change the story** — the result that would force a different storyline.

The ghost deck is about 20 percent complete — mostly titles. It is a bet, and the storyline
step is free to break it when the evidence disagrees.
