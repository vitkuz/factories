# How the problem statement is shaped

The problem statement is the contract for the whole study. It is written before any search,
and every later step reads it. "A well-defined problem is a problem half-solved."

## Sections, in this order

1. **Topic** — the topic as the person gave it, one line, unchanged.
2. **Decision maker** — who decides, their role, what they value, what they fear. When the
   audience given is generic ("an executive"), infer the most useful concrete decision maker for
   the topic and say you inferred it.
3. **Decision** — the choice this study informs, as a choice: "enter or not", "build, buy or
   partner", "where to invest the next $X". If the topic implies no decision, pick the most
   plausible one and say so; a study with no decision behind it becomes a tour of the topic.
4. **Governing question** — one sentence, SMART: Specific, Measurable, Action-oriented,
   Relevant, Time-bound. Pattern: *How can / Should <actor> <achieve a measurable outcome> by
   <date>?* A reader at the end must be able to tell whether it was answered.
5. **Situation and complication** — the first half of SCQA. Situation: three to five facts the
   reader already agrees with. Complication: what changed, and why it forces a decision now.
   Mark every statement here as believed-to-be-true background; no URLs yet.
6. **Criteria for success** — how the decision maker will judge the answer: the quantitative
   target, the qualitative criteria (timing, risk, capability) and what failure looks like.
7. **Scope** — geography, time frame, segments in scope; and **out of scope**, one line each,
   including what a reader might expect but will not get.
8. **Constraints** — public sources only; the accuracy needed (order of magnitude vs ±10%);
   anything the question must not assume.
9. **Key questions a reader will ask** — five to eight, the ones the decision maker would ask
   in the first meeting. These feed the issue tree; they are not yet MECE.
10. **Day-one answer** — the answer you would bet on today, in one or two sentences, written
    as a claim with a number where possible. Label it clearly as a hypothesis. Add the one
    thing that, if true, would most likely overturn it.

## The checklist before you finish

- Is it a question, not a topic? Is there a named decision?
- Is the scope too narrow (it presumes the answer) or too broad (it boils the ocean)?
- Could someone at the end say yes, no, or a number?
- Is the day-one answer falsifiable — could evidence prove it wrong?

## Example (shortened)

- Decision maker: CTO and CEO of a 300-person B2B software company.
- Decision: whether to build an agent-infrastructure product line, and which segment first.
- Governing question: Should the company launch an agent observability and governance product
  by Q4 2027, and can it reach $20M ARR within three years of launch?
- Day-one answer: Yes — observability and governance will monetize before autonomous-agent
  marketplaces, because enterprises moving pilots into production hit control problems first.
  Most likely to overturn it: cloud providers bundling these features for free.
