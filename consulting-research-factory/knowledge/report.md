# How the report is shaped

The report follows the anatomy of public McKinsey Global Institute and McKinsey insight reports
(2023–2026): At a glance, introduction, chapters with claim headings, numbered exhibits,
implications for leaders, methodology, sources. It is written for a decision maker with five
minutes who can go deeper if they want, and for a reviewer who will check every number.

## Structure, in this order

1. **Title** — `# Main phrase: subtitle`, then a one-sentence *dek* in italics carrying the
   headline number. Then one line: audience, geography, time frame, date.
2. **At a glance** — exactly five bullets. Each opens with a **bold one-sentence claim**, then
   one or two sentences with the number and the mechanism (40–90 words per bullet). At least four
   carry a number or range. The arc: headline finding → quantified findings → mechanism → the
   implication or call to action as the last bullet. A reader who stops here has the answer.
3. **Executive summary** — the governing thought first, then one paragraph per key message.
   Each paragraph opens with a **bold lead sentence** (the chapter's claim) followed by three to
   five sentences of evidence, referring to exhibits by number. Include the recommendations in
   brief. One to two pages. It must stand alone.
4. **Introduction** — SCQA in prose: situation, complication, the question. Then scope (what the
   study covers and does not), the decision maker it is written for, and a roadmap: "Chapter 1
   shows… Chapter 2 examines…".
5. **Chapters, one per key message** — `## 1. <full-sentence claim heading>`.
   - Open with the chapter's answer in one or two sentences, then why it matters.
   - Sections under it are `### <claim heading>`, one per supporting point.
   - Place each exhibit block where the storyline puts it, **copied verbatim** from the exhibits
     file, and refer to it in the text ("Exhibit 3 shows…") with what it means for the reader.
   - A sidebar or case example, where the evidence has one, as a blockquote headed
     `> **Case example: <title>**`, with its result quantified.
6. **What this means for <decision maker>** — the recommendations, each: bold imperative, why
   (tracing to the chapter), impact as a range, first step, timing. Then **Key questions for
   leaders**: four to six questions the decision maker should be able to answer.
7. **Risks, uncertainties and what would change our view** — the conditions that must hold, the
   strongest counter-argument, and the leading indicators to watch.
8. **Hypotheses tested** — a table: each hypothesis, its verdict, the decisive evidence ids.
   Say which day-one beliefs turned out wrong.
9. **Methodology and limitations** — the question and scope; the research approach
   (hypothesis-driven, three lenses, evidence base); sources searched and when; how derived
   numbers were computed (formulas, market-sizing routes and how they reconciled); key
   assumptions and what happens if they are wrong; known gaps; the date data is as of; and the
   statement that this is desk research from public sources, not a forecast.
10. **Sources** — numbered list: `[EV-012] Publisher, "Title", date. URL (tier)`. Only sources the
    report cites.

## Writing rules

- **Answer first**, in every chapter, section and paragraph. No section builds up to its point.
- **Headings are claims**, with a subject and a verb, usually with a number.
- **Cite as you go:** every factual sentence ends with its evidence ids in parentheses `(EV-012)`
  or `(EV-012, EV-031)`. Derived figures cite their inputs and say "we estimate".
- **Numbers, not adjectives.** "Large" → "12 percent"; "recently" → "since Q3 2025"; "many firms"
  → "seven of ten firms surveyed". Every number is in the number register, with the same value,
  unit and year everywhere it appears.
- **Calibrated language:** forward numbers as ranges or named scenarios; "could", "up to", "as
  much as", and the likelihood terms from the method file. Potential is not a forecast.
  Conditionals get an explicit "if".
- **Short sentences** (15–20 words on average, none over 40), active voice, one idea per paragraph,
  plain words, acronyms defined once.
- In body text write "percent"; the "%" sign is for exhibits and tables.
- **Only what the evidence carries.** The writer does not search. A fact missing from the evidence
  base is a known gap and is said to be one.
- Length follows the evidence: typically 3,000–6,000 words and 6–12 exhibits.

## On a revision pass

Fix exactly the points the review assigns to the writer; leave everything else untouched. Open the
file with a `## Review points addressed` section listing the point numbers you fixed and how. The
clerk removes that section from the final package.
